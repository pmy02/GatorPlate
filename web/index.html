01 The gap at SF State

About 3,450 SF State students likely qualify for CalFresh and aren't getting it.

up to $12.7M a year

Up to $12.7 million a year in food money — CalFresh alone, at the one-person maximum.

≈2,580 already receive it ≈3,450 not yet

Estimate based on UC eligibility research applied to SF State enrollment: 33% of UC undergraduates and 7% of UC graduate students were eligible for CalFresh (California Policy Lab, Fall 2019 data, published Aug 2024), applied to SF State's 17,607 undergraduates and 3,114 graduate students (Fall 2025), minus the 14.3% of SF State undergraduates who already received CalFresh in 2023–24 (California Policy Lab, Aug 2026) and graduate students at the UC rate. "Up to" assumes the $306 one-person maximum for 12 months; the average benefit is lower.

  1. Enrollment: 17,607 undergraduates and 3,114 graduate students, Fall 2025 (SF State Facts).
  2. Eligible: 33% of UC undergraduates and 7% of UC graduate students, Fall 2019 (California Policy Lab, "Filling the Gap," Aug 2024) → ≈6,030 at SF State.
  3. Already receiving: 14.3% of SF State undergraduates, 2023–24 (California Policy Lab, Aug 2026); graduate students at the UC rate of 27% of those eligible → ≈2,580.
  4. Up to $306/month: CalFresh maximum for one person from Oct 1, 2026 (CDSS ACIN I-40-26).
  5. Average is lower: participating UC undergraduates averaged $161/month when the maximum was $194 (California Policy Lab, Feb 2025) — about $10.5M/yr at that ratio.
  6. Graduate students are estimated with UC rates (7% eligible, 27% of them receiving). SF State's own data show 13.6% of graduate students already receive CalFresh — higher than this method assumes — so the ~160 graduate students in this estimate are the least certain part.
  7. Likely conservative: since June 1, 2026, half-time CSU bachelor's students meet the student rule (CDSS ACL 26-25); the 2019 rate predates this.

02 How it works

The AI understands and plans. Rules do the math. A person confirms.

A short conversation instead of a screening form. Two AI steps sit at the center: a language model that understands free speech, and a planner that decides what to ask next.

Highlighted: the two AI steps

  1. 1

    Student

    You talk

    By phone (English) or in your browser (English or Spanish). GatorPlate gets text, never audio. Code sets noise and line checks (“hello?”) aside before the AI sees them.

    AI's part Speech recognition (AI) turns your voice into text; GatorPlate gets text only.

  2. 2

    AI · Language model

    Understands

    Turns free speech — any wording, corrections, several answers at once — into the exact facts the rules need.

    AI's part Fills each fact with the student's own quote and flags what it isn't sure of.

  3. 3

    Code

    Checked against your words

    A rule parser reads every sentence too. If it disagrees with the AI on an amount, the answer is marked unsure for a person to check, and a key amount can be confirmed on the call.

    AI's part Its output is checked: a number the student never said is dropped.

  4. 4

    Code

    Rules do the math

    A dated CalFresh table (Oct 2026 – Sep 2027) computes every dollar, with a source for each step.

    AI's part None, by design: the AI never does the math.

  5. 5

    AI · Planner

    Picks the next question

    Before each optional question, it re-runs the rules for every candidate answer — a value-of-information search. Core questions follow a fixed order.

    AI's part Asks only if an answer could change the result or move the estimate by more than $50 a month — at most two, starting with the most decisive.

  6. 6

    Person

    A person confirms

    Uncertain answers become yellow lines; a case can't be marked reviewed until each is checked. The county decides.

    To check Monthly rent — “around 800, I think”

    AI's part Its “unsure” flags are what the person checks.

  7. 7

    Student

    Your card

    The estimate, answers for the application, documents, interview prep — and other help CalFresh may open.

    AI's part None, by design: code builds the card from the rules result.

Steps 1–5 repeat every turn. After every answer, the planner re-plans — until it moves on to the result.

Performance

Measured Oct 2, 2026 · simulated students, text-level

  • 24/24

    off-script and noisy calls with the real language model reached the right amount

    24 runs: 15 variations of 3 simulated students (one in Spanish) plus the Maria demo script — noise on every turn, a TV in the room, cut-off words, corrections, several answers at once. 3 of 113 model calls timed out; the rule parser carried on and the amount was still right.

  • 0

    wrong values reached a result

    Same 24 runs. One noisy word (“nine”) was saved as $9 but marked unsure, and the confirm question fixed it. No call was ended by noise.

  • 100%

    of saved quotes are the student's own words

    Enforced in code and tested: a quote not found in what was said turns the answer “unsure”; a number the student never said is dropped.

  • 3,200+

    automated tests pass

    3,054 Python and 176 JavaScript tests, with a stand-in for the language model. Plus 28/28 end-to-end and 38/38 adversarial scripts (prompt injection, a spoken Social Security number, a crisis in Spanish).

  • 78/78

    hand-computed CalFresh cases match the rules engine exactly

    Every result field compared. The other programs: 14/14 hand-computed cases.

  • 7

    questions in Maria's demo call, out of 37 answer fields

    They filled 12 answers. The planner skipped 2 that couldn't change her result and asked 1 that could move it by $151 a month. Average across 59 simulated calls: 5.9.

  • ≈1.5 s

    median for the AI to understand an answer (language-model step)

    Local runs of the current code, n = 32 model calls. Rules, planner, sentences and checks add a median of about 5 ms per answer (n = 72). Phone and network time not included.

A Laws & standards

Sources read Oct 2, 2026. Each citation links to the law's text. Our reading, not legal advice.

Laws and standards: what each requires, what GatorPlate does today, and its status
Law or standard What it requires What GatorPlate does today Status
California Invasion of Privacy Act Penal Code §631, §632 Listening in on or recording a confidential call needs the consent of all parties. Before any question, the call says it is a student-built AI, not an official SF State service, that an AI turns speech into text, and that the call audio isn't recorded. Then it asks: say yes or press one. A no, or two unclear answers, ends the call; only the fact that it was declined is kept. Designed for it Built for all-party consent. Pilot: legal review of the consent wording.
California bot disclosure Bus. & Prof. Code §17940–17941 (SB 1001) An online bot may not hide that it is a bot to push a sale or sway a vote. Says “AI” in its opening sentence, on the phone and on the web. “Are you a robot?”, “Is this recorded?” and “Is this official?” get a true answer at any point. Outside scope, as we read it Nothing is sold and there is no vote. We disclose anyway.
CCPA / CPRA Civil Code §1798.100 ff. Duties fall on for-profit businesses above a revenue or data-volume threshold. No revenue, no ads, no data sales. Keeps answers with short quotes, not the conversation; never receives the caller's phone number; never asks for a name. Delete by voice (“delete my data”) or on the card. Outside scope, as we read it Not a for-profit business. We follow its collect-less-and-delete ideas anyway.
CalFresh confidentiality Welf. & Inst. Code §10850; 7 CFR 272.1(c) Agencies running CalFresh must keep applicant and recipient records confidential and use them only for the program. Receives no county or state records and sends nothing to the county. Keeps no list of recipients. Students apply on BenefitsCal themselves. Outside scope, as we read it An agency rule; we hold no agency records. Sharing data with the county would need its own review.
FERPA 20 U.S.C. §1232g; 34 CFR Part 99 Protects education records kept by a school or someone acting for it. The pilot uses no student records, so it needs no student-records integration. Pilot: review needed SF State's data and accessibility review comes before a pilot.
TCPA 47 U.S.C. §227(b); FCC ruling, Feb. 8, 2024 Calls made with an artificial or prerecorded voice — AI voices included — need prior consent. Only answers calls a student places. No outbound calls, texts or return calls: none are built, and the reply guard blocks promising them. Designed for it Inbound calls only.
CSU name use Education Code §89005.5 No one may use the CSU or a campus name to suggest affiliation or endorsement without the Trustees' permission. Every call and web chat opens with “a student-built AI assistant, not an official SF State service.” This site says it is not an official SF State, county or CalFresh service. No SF State logos, seals or colors. Designed for it A factual description only. Any adoption starts with SF State's own review.
Accessibility ADA Title II web rule (DOJ 2024; WCAG 2.1 AA; larger public entities by Apr. 26, 2027) · WCAG 2.2 AA A campus must offer web content and apps that people with disabilities can use. The phone path needs no screen, app or login, and speaks the card code. Typing always works on the web. The card targets WCAG 2.2 AA: 117 color pairs checked (4.5:1 text, 3:1 interface), keyboard use, screen-reader labels, 200% zoom, reflow at 320 px. Designed for it WCAG 2.2 AA targeted, not audited. Pilot: SF State accessibility review. Calls from TTY users aren't specially handled yet.
HIPAA 45 CFR Parts 160, 164 Covers health plans, health-care clearinghouses and providers that bill electronically. None of these. Medi-Cal appears only as a box on the same application. No health or disability details are stored. Outside scope, as we read it Not a covered entity.
Truthful claims FTC Act §5; Bus. & Prof. Code §17500 Claims must not mislead; “up to” must not read as what everyone gets. Every amount says it is an estimate and the agency decides. Ceilings say “up to.” The $12.7M is an upper bound for CalFresh alone, with its method and sources on this page. A reply guard blocks promises of savings or payouts. Designed for it Wording rules are tested in code.

B Risks & how we handle them

Risks: how GatorPlate handles each, the evidence, and what is still open
Risk How we handle it Evidence Still open
Privacy Consent before questions. No audio stored, no full transcript, no caller number received. A case keeps answers with short quotes (80 characters at most). The language model sees only the current question and the last two sentences, with ID-like digits removed. Students can delete their case by voice or on the card. Tests: a declined call keeps no answers — only that it was declined; “delete my data” removes the case. The consent wording has not been reviewed by a lawyer yet.
Security The AI can't set a number. Phone requests are signed; the coordinator console needs a passcode; card links are unguessable and expire (7 days; spoken code 24 hours); requests are rate-limited; logs hold no conversation content; pages load nothing from third parties. Tests: routes, logs, request signatures. Scripts: prompt injection. SF State's data review comes before a pilot.
Accessibility Any phone, no app or login. The card code is spoken. Typing on the web. WCAG 2.2 AA targets on the card. Tests: 117 color pairs pass the contrast check (Oct 2, 2026). Scripts: a question about TTY services is noted, never promised. No WCAG audit yet. Calls from TTY users aren't specially handled yet.
Data accuracy One dated rules table (Oct 1, 2026 – Sep 30, 2027), each value with its source. No estimate outside those dates. Tests: 78/78 hand-computed CalFresh cases and 14/14 other-program cases (Oct 2, 2026). A new table is needed from Oct 1, 2027.
Bias & language One rules engine for English and Spanish; results reported per language. Spanish works on the web and on the card; the phone line is English. Runs: simulated calls with a stand-in model, English 48/48 and Spanish 11/11 (Oct 2, 2026); 1 real-model Spanish run, right amount. Real accents and real speech recognition not tested yet. The phone line is English only.
Over-reliance Every result is an estimate; GatorPlate never gives a “no” on eligibility. Uncertain answers become yellow lines, and the server won't mark a case reviewed until a person checks each one. It never applies or signs for anyone. Tests: the review stays locked until every yellow line is checked. Scripts: asking for a “no”; asking it to apply. Not yet tried with real students or coordinators.
Crisis A mention of self-harm, in English or Spanish, gets 988 and 911 at once, before anything else. The AI and word lists both listen, so it works even if the language model is down. Nothing from that sentence is quoted. Tests: crisis words in both languages. Scripts: a crisis in English; a crisis in Spanish. With the model down, only the word lists listen; indirect wording can be missed.
Immigration safety Never asks for a Social Security number or immigration status. A volunteered status isn't stored or kept as context; the student is pointed to the coordinator or legal aid. SSN- or card-like digits are removed before the AI sees the text. Tests: digit redaction. Scripts: a spoken Social Security number; an immigration question. Tested with text scripts, not real speech.
AI errors & invented numbers The model only fills answers. Each quote must appear in what the student said and each number must have been said — otherwise the answer is marked unsure or dropped. Amounts come from the rules table. Risky numbers (“fifteen or fifty?”) are read back. If the model is slow or down, a rule parser and closed questions carry on. Tests: merge, model faults. Scripts: “fifteen hundred” read back. Runs: 24/24 real-model noisy runs (above). In those runs, 3 of 113 model calls timed out; the rule parser carried on.
Noise & off-script speech Noise, a TV in the room or “hello?” is never saved or counted as an answer and makes no model call; the question is asked again. Side questions are noted for the coordinator. Long silence gets a closed question. Tests: noise turns. Scripts: three silences; a side question; mixed English and Spanish. Real phone lines and speech recognition not tested yet.
